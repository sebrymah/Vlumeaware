# Part A — Brochure

*Audience: all employees. Format: two-sided A5 or single A4 sheet. Word count: 500.*

---

## Security is a team sport

Every organisation holds something worth stealing — money, customer data, a reputation built over years. **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** is the internationally recognised standard that says, in effect: *prove you protect it.* It asks us to work out what could go wrong, decide what to do about it, and be able to show our reasoning. Certification is not a plaque for the reception wall. It is evidence — to clients, regulators and partners — that security here is deliberate rather than accidental.

Now the uncomfortable part. Most attackers do not break down the door. They email it. Phishing remains one of the most common ways a breach begins, and a single click can undo a great deal of careful engineering. That is why ISO 27001 sets specific controls for awareness and training (A.6.3), for reporting incidents (A.5.24–A.5.26), and for how information is classified, stored and shared.

Think of it this way. Our firewalls and access controls are the locks. **You are the person who notices someone testing the handle.**

### Three things ISO 27001 expects of you

**1. Know what you are protecting.** Information here is classified — Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted. The label tells you how to store it, share it and dispose of it.

**2. Protect your access.** Unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, and never approving a login prompt you did not start.

**3. Report quickly, without fear.** If something feels wrong — an odd email, a lost phone, data sent to the wrong person — say so immediately. Early reporting is containment. Ours is a no-blame culture: we would far rather hear about a mistake in five minutes than discover it in five months.

### What phishing actually looks like

Most phishing leans on three pressures: **urgency** ("within 24 hours"), **authority** (your bank, your regulator, your IT department) and **curiosity** ("Confidential — Q3 Salary Review"). The lures that reach our market are specific: a supplier changing bank details the day before the invoice run, a payroll confirmation the week salaries are processed, a re-verification notice dressed up as a tax or regulatory demand.

You do not need to be a security expert to catch these. You need one habit: **stop, check, verify through a channel you already trust.** Hover over a link before clicking it. Confirm a change of bank details by calling a number you already hold — never one supplied in the email.

### What happens next

You will receive short training videos and knowledge checks. With your consent, and only ever against your own work address, you may also receive realistic simulations. If you click one, you get an immediate, friendly explanation and the exact training for that attack. **There is no penalty for clicking** — only the chance to get better.

Passing your knowledge check earns a certificate you can keep. More importantly, it builds the instinct that protects all of us. ISO 27001 gives us the framework. You give it force.

*Questions? Contact the security awareness team.*

# Part B — Programme video script

**Title:** *The Email That Costs Everything*
**Runtime:** 4 min 30 s · **Format:** 16:9, captioned · **Tone:** calm, direct, non-patronising
**Audience:** all staff, played at induction and at programme launch
**Voiceover target:** 140 words per minute. Total VO ≈ 630 words.

## Production notes

- **Cast for realism, not drama.** The member of staff who clicks should be competent and likeable. A viewer who feels stupid stops watching; a viewer who thinks "that could have been me" keeps watching.
- **Show the email on screen at real size.** Phishing lands because it looks ordinary. Full-screen mock-ups at readable scale, not stylised graphics.
- **Blur real credentials.** Any mock-up that shows a password field uses greyed placeholder text.
- **Localise per market.** The invoice-run, payroll-week and regulator lures can be swapped for equivalents without changing the structure.

## Shot list and script

**0:00–0:18 · Cold open**

*Visual:* Close-up, screen recording. An inbox. A new message slides in. We hold on it — no music sting, no alarm. We hear a keyboard, then a single click. Cut to black.

*VO:* "This is the most expensive click most organisations will ever make. It looked completely ordinary. That is the whole point."

**0:18–0:45 · The problem, stated plainly**

*Visual:* Split screen — left, a locked server room door; right, the same inbox. The camera slowly favours the inbox.

*VO:* "Attackers rarely break down the door. They email it. Phishing is one of the most common ways a breach starts, and it does not need a clever target. It needs one person, on a busy afternoon, moving quickly."

**0:45–1:30 · What ISO 27001 is, in plain English**

*Visual:* Clean motion graphics. Four words build on screen: **Identify. Protect. Prove. Improve.** Then a document labelled "Annex A" with ten topic labels flying into place.

*VO:* "ISO/IEC 27001:2022 is the international standard for information security management. In plain terms, it asks us to do four things: work out what could go wrong, put controls in place, prove that we did, and keep getting better. It also sets expectations of every one of us — in awareness and training, in how we handle information, and in how quickly we report a problem."

**1:30–2:20 · The three pressures**

*Visual:* Three mock emails, one per pressure, each with the trigger word highlighted. Caption on screen: **URGENCY · AUTHORITY · CURIOSITY.**

*VO:* "Almost every phishing email pushes one of three buttons. **Urgency** — act in the next hour or lose access. **Authority** — it appears to come from your bank, your regulator, or your IT department. **Curiosity** — a confidential document, a salary review, a message you were not expecting. Recognise the pressure and you have already caught most attacks."

**2:20–3:25 · The five checks**

*Visual:* The same email returns. Each check is stamped onto the screen as the narrator names it. On the second check, we hover a link and the true destination appears in the status bar, clearly different from the link text.

*VO:* "Five checks catch almost every phishing email. **One:** does the sender address match who it claims to be? **Two:** hover the link — does the real destination match the text you are being shown? **Three:** is there pressure to act now? **Four:** were you expecting this request at all? **Five:** is there an attachment you did not ask for? If any check fails, stop. Then verify through a channel you already trust — a number you already have, or the person themselves, not the contact details in the email."

**3:25–4:10 · Reporting, and no blame**

*Visual:* Someone hesitates at their desk, then clicks a "Report phishing" button. A colleague nods. Tone shifts — warmer lighting.

*VO:* "If you do click, say so immediately. Reporting is not an admission of failure; it is the fastest way to contain a problem. Our culture is no-blame: we would rather know in five minutes than find out five months later. When you report a suspicious email, you protect the person in the next office who would have clicked it."

**4:10–4:30 · Close**

*Visual:* Return to the opening inbox. This time the cursor hovers the link, hesitates, and clicks **Report phishing**. Cut to the programme title card.

*VO:* "One habit: stop, check, verify. That is what ISO 27001 looks like in practice — and it is the only control that works at the speed of your inbox."

*For LMS embedding and induction decks. Same structure, compressed.*

# Part C — Quiz bank

**30 quizzes · 150 questions.** Ten topics, three tiers, five questions each. Every question has a single defensible correct answer, a short explanation of why it is right and why the tempting wrong answer is not, and the ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A control that the topic supports.

## How to use the bank

| Tier | Name | Assignment rule | Pass mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundation | Everyone, on first assignment | 80% |
| 2 | Practitioner | After a click, or after passing Tier 1 | 80% |
| 3 | Advanced | Repeat clickers, high-risk roles, on request | 80% |

- **Scoring.** All three tiers use an 80% pass mark (four of five correct). Retakes are unlimited, and the platform records the best score.
- **Answer keys are balanced.** Across all 150 questions the key sits at roughly 40 A / 40 B / 35 C / 35 D, and within each five-question quiz the answers are spread. The legacy Foundation bank answered "B" to 38 of its 50 questions, which meant a learner could pass several quizzes by always choosing B; option order has been rebalanced so that guessing now scores at chance. No question wording or answer was changed to achieve this — only the order of the options.
- **Difficulty is honest.** Tier 1 is baseline knowledge. Tier 2 applies it to a role you actually do. Tier 3 tests judgement where the situation has already partly gone wrong and the *order* of your actions is what matters.
- **No unexplained jargon.** Technical terms are spelled out in plain English at first use. Appendix 1 carries the full glossary.
- **Question IDs** are `Q<topic>.<tier>.<number>`, so `Q7.3.2` is Topic 7, Tier 3, question 2. Use these IDs for LMS imports, item analysis, and any dispute about a single question.
- **An item that fails repeatedly should be fixed, not re-run.** If a module is failed by more than a quarter of a cohort who passed the video, review the question before blaming the cohort.

## Topic 1 — Phishing & social engineering

*ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A: A.6.3, A.5.7, A.8.7*

### Topic 1 — Phishing & social engineering: Tier 1 — Foundation

**Q1.1.1** An email says your account will be closed in one hour unless you "verify" it via a link. What is the safest first step?
- A. Stop and verify through a known channel, such as the official website or IT
- B. Click the link quickly so you do not lose access
- C. Reply to the email asking if it is genuine
- D. Forward it to a colleague to click and check
**Answer: A** — Urgency is a pressure tactic. Never act on the email’s own links — verify independently through a channel you already trust.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q1.1.2** Which of these is the strongest sign a message is a phishing attempt?
- A. It is addressed to you by name
- B. The link text and the actual link destination do not match
- C. It has a company logo
- D. It was sent during working hours
**Answer: B** — Attackers hide malicious URLs behind trusted-looking text. Hover to reveal the true destination before clicking.
`ISO: A.5.7 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q1.1.3** What is "social engineering"?
- A. A method for designing secure software
- B. A type of firewall configuration
- C. Manipulating people into breaking security or revealing information
- D. The study of social media algorithms
**Answer: C** — Social engineering targets human trust and habits rather than technical flaws — it is why awareness matters.
`ISO: A.8.7 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q1.1.4** A vendor emails new bank details for an overdue invoice, marked urgent. What should you do?
- A. Update the payment details as requested
- B. Pay a small amount first to test the account
- C. Ignore it — invoices are not a security issue
- D. Verify the change by calling the vendor on a number you already have on file
**Answer: D** — Bank-detail changes are a classic business email compromise. Always confirm out-of-band using contact details you already hold.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q1.1.5** You clicked a suspicious link before realising it may be phishing. What is the right action?
- A. Report it to IT/security immediately so they can respond
- B. Say nothing in case you get in trouble
- C. Delete the email and hope for the best
- D. Turn off your computer and wait a week
**Answer: A** — Fast reporting limits damage. Organisations expect and encourage reports — you will never be blamed for reporting.
`ISO: A.5.7 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

### Topic 1 — Phishing & social engineering: Tier 2 — Practitioner

**Q1.2.1** It is 4:20 pm on a Tuesday and you work in the finance team. An email arrives with the Managing Director's name as the sender and a free webmail address behind it. It asks for an urgent payment of ₦4.8 million to a new beneficiary, is marked "confidential", and ends with "do not call me, I am in a board meeting until late". What is the best action?
- A. Reply to the email asking the MD to confirm the beneficiary name and account number before you raise the payment
- B. Raise the payment, because the sender name matches the MD and a delayed payment may damage an important relationship
- C. Follow the company's payment-approval process and confirm the request with the MD using the number already saved in your contacts or the approved call-back list
- D. Forward the email to a colleague in finance and ask whether they also think it looks genuine
**Answer: C** — A payment request that rushes you, hides behind confidentiality, and blocks the usual call-back is the pattern of business email compromise (BEC — an attacker impersonating a trusted colleague or supplier), so the defence is to verify through a channel the attacker does not control. Option A is the tempting one because a reply feels like confirmation, but the reply simply goes back to the same fraudulent mailbox, and the request is never checked against reality.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q1.2.2** You process payroll and salaries are due in two days. An email arrives from an employee's personal address, quoting their staff number, and asks you to change their salary account to a new bank account because "the old account has been frozen". What should you do first?
- A. Confirm the change with the employee in person or on a number you already hold for them, and have a second person approve the update before it is loaded into payroll
- B. Update the account straight away, since the request quotes the correct staff number and the employee is clearly anxious about being paid
- C. Ask the employee to resend the request from their work email address and then process the change the same day
- D. Hold the salary until the employee comes to the office, then pay the balance in cash instead
**Answer: A** — Salary account changes are a favourite target because one successful change can divert a whole month's pay, so the request must be confirmed out of band and approved by a second pair of eyes. Option B is the tempting quick fix, but quoting a staff number proves nothing — that number appears on payslips, ID badges, and old email threads, so it is the easiest detail for an attacker to learn.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q1.2.3** You are on the IT service desk. A user calls to say an email claiming to be from IT is asking them to re-enter their password on a page that "looks like our company portal". They have not clicked anything yet. What is the best response?
- A. Tell the user to delete the email and carry on with their work, since nothing was clicked
- B. Ask the user to forward the email to your personal mailbox so you can open it later and check the link
- C. Open the link yourself from your own desk computer to see whether the sign-in page is the genuine one
- D. Ask the user to forward the message to the security mailbox or press the "Report Phishing" button without clicking, then log the incident and alert other staff who received the same message
**Answer: D** — Reporting without clicking preserves the evidence, gets the link blocked for everyone, and warns colleagues who may be one click away from handing over their credentials. Option A is the tempting "no harm done" response, but a link that reaches an inbox and is never reported stays available to the next person, and the security team loses the warning it needs.
`ISO: A.8.7 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q1.2.4** You are an operations supervisor. This morning an email signed by "IT Helpdesk" tells all staff to update their VPN client (virtual private network — a protected remote connection into company systems) by clicking a link before 6:00 pm. An hour earlier your real IT manager emailed everyone warning that a fake IT message is going around. What is the best action?
- A. Click the link and install the update, because you need the VPN to finish month-end work from home
- B. Delete the "IT Helpdesk" email, report it through the phishing report button, and warn your team to do the same, treating the IT manager's earlier warning as the reliable source
- C. Click the link but only on your personal phone, so the company laptop stays protected
- D. Reply to the "IT Helpdesk" email and ask them to confirm that the download link is genuine
**Answer: B** — An internal warning issued before the request is exactly the context that turns a routine-looking IT email into a known campaign, so acting on the trusted warning and reporting the message protects the whole team. Option D is the tempting polite route, but replying only reaches the attacker and the answer you receive back will always say "yes, it is genuine".
`ISO: A.5.7 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q1.2.5** A message arrives on your personal WhatsApp from an unknown number using your MD's photograph as its profile picture. The sender says they are in a meeting, cannot talk, and need you to buy four ₦50,000 gift cards for a client and send the codes immediately. What should you do?
- A. Do not buy anything; confirm with the MD through a number you already have for them, and report the approach to IT or security as an attempted impersonation
- B. Buy one card now to keep the client relationship safe, then check with the MD later when they are free
- C. Reply on WhatsApp asking the sender to prove who they are by naming the company's current project
- D. Share the message in the staff group so colleagues can decide together whether the request is genuine
**Answer: A** — Gift-card requests work because codes are handed over instantly and cannot be traced or recovered, so the safe move is to stop and verify on a channel the attacker does not control. Option B is the tempting "half-measure", but even one card is money gone for good, and the attacker will simply ask for more once the first codes work.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

### Topic 1 — Phishing & social engineering: Tier 3 — Advanced

**Q1.3.1** A finance officer entered their email password on a convincing sign-in page two weeks ago but changed the password afterwards and told nobody. This morning, the day of the monthly invoice run, the Chief Financial Officer receives a payment instruction from that officer's real mailbox, written in their usual style. The officer is on leave. What is the most important first action?
- A. Change the officer's password again and let the invoice run proceed as normal, since the mailbox is now secure
- B. Call the officer at home and ask whether they sent the instruction before deciding what to do
- C. Tell the CFO to approve the payment but flag it for review after month-end, so the invoicing calendar is not disrupted
- D. Treat the account as actively compromised: pause the payment run, disable the mailbox and revoke its active sessions and any mail-forwarding or inbox rules, then escalate to the incident response lead
**Answer: D** — An attacker who has held a mailbox for two weeks usually leaves behind forwarding rules and live sessions that survive a password change, so the account must be cut off and the payment process stopped before anything else is decided. Option A is the tempting response because changing a password feels like a completed fix, but it leaves the attacker's hidden access and the fraudulent instruction in play on the busiest payment day of the month.
`ISO: A.5.7 · Tier 3 · Advanced · Difficulty 3/3`

**Q1.3.2** It is 4:45 pm on the last working day before a filing deadline. You receive a "final re-verification notice" styled as a demand from the Federal Inland Revenue Service (FIRS — Nigeria's tax authority). It warns that your company's tax profile will be suspended within two hours unless you confirm the company details through the link provided. One colleague says you must act now to avoid penalties; another says wait. What should you do?
- A. Open the link and complete the re-verification, because missing a FIRS deadline would expose the company to penalties and the site shows a padlock symbol
- B. Do not use the link; verify the notice through an independent, known FIRS contact or your tax adviser, and log the attempt, because a short deadline plus an emailed payment or verification link is the pressure tactic itself
- C. Pay a small "administrative fee" through the link to buy time while you confirm the notice with your accountant
- D. Forward the notice to the whole finance team and ask whether anyone recognises it before you decide
**Answer: B** — Genuine regulators can be reached and confirmed through published channels, so the correct move is to break the time pressure by verifying independently rather than trusting the message's own countdown. Option A is the tempting one because the deadline and the padlock both feel like proof, but a padlock only means the connection is encrypted — anyone, including a fraudster, can obtain one for their own site.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 3 · Advanced · Difficulty 3/3`

**Q1.3.3** Your accounts payable team paid a supplier invoice to a "new" account after the supplier's mailbox was quietly taken over; the emails were polite, on-brand, and answered questions convincingly. Two days later the real supplier calls to say the invoice is unpaid and their email was hacked. The money left the account by instant transfer this morning, and it is now 6:00 pm. What is the correct first escalation step?
- A. Wait until the bank opens tomorrow morning, because an instant transfer that has already left the account cannot be recalled
- B. Email the account holder who received the money and demand that it be returned within 24 hours
- C. Contact the bank's fraud desk and your incident response lead immediately with the transaction details to attempt a recall or freeze, preserve the emails as evidence, and report the crime to the relevant authorities
- D. Delete the fraudulent invoice, block the supplier's domain, and write a lesson-learned note for the finance team
**Answer: C** — Minutes matter after a fraudulent transfer, because a fast report to the bank gives the best chance of freezing funds before they are moved on, and evidence preservation supports both recovery and any police or regulatory follow-up. Option A is tempting because instant payments feel irreversible, but recall and beneficiary-freeze processes do exist and they only start when the bank is told — a night of silence guarantees the loss.
`ISO: A.5.7 · Tier 3 · Advanced · Difficulty 3/3`

**Q1.3.4** You manage HR. An email signed by a "National Data Protection Commission officer" correctly quotes your company's NDPR registration (Nigeria Data Protection Regulation — Nigeria's data protection rules) and demands that a spreadsheet of staff names, salaries, and bank account numbers be uploaded to a portal link within 24 hours to avoid a fine. Your assistant says the deadline is real and you must comply; your IT lead says stop. What is the best action?
- A. Do not upload; verify the request using the Commission's published contact details and route it to your Data Protection Officer, because a genuine regulator does not demand bulk personal data through an unsolicited link under a 24-hour threat
- B. Upload the names and salaries but leave out the bank account numbers, to reduce the exposure while still meeting the deadline
- C. Reply to the sender with your company's registration number and ask them to confirm their identity in writing
- D. Upload the file now and inform the Data Protection Officer afterwards if the portal looks suspicious
**Answer: A** — The correct principle is that personal data leaves the company only through a verified, authorised request and an approved channel, no matter how official the message looks or how short the deadline. Option B is the tempting compromise because it feels like partial safety, but it still hands a large set of personal data to an unverified party and confirms that your team can be pressured into complying.
`ISO: A.6.3 · Tier 3 · Advanced · Difficulty 3/3`

**Q1.3.5** A finance officer receives a phone call from a voice that sounds exactly like the Chief Financial Officer, asking about a confidential acquisition. Minutes later an email from the CFO's address repeats the request: transfer ₦12 million to a named "legal firm" today and keep it secret. Both the call and the email agree, and the voice is familiar and natural. What is the correct response?
- A. Make the transfer and mark it confidential, since the CFO authorised it verbally and in writing and the acquisition must not leak
- B. Ask the CFO's personal assistant to confirm that the payment is genuine, then release the funds
- C. Delay the payment without explanation, and wait for someone to ask about it
- D. Follow the standard dual-approval process and verify with the CFO through a pre-agreed channel, treating the call and email as unverified claims, then report the attempt as a suspected voice-cloning attack
**Answer: D** — A cloned voice and a spoofed sender name are both synthetic, so the only reliable check is the approval process plus a call-back on a number your team already holds. Option B is the tempting shortcut because the assistant sounds like an independent witness, but the assistant is reachable through the same compromised or spoofed channels and can be deceived by the same request.

*ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A: A.5.17, A.8.5*

### Topic 2 — Passwords & multi-factor authentication: Tier 1 — Foundation

**Q2.1.1** Which makes the strongest password?
- A. A short word with a number, like "Cat7"
- B. A long, unique passphrase such as "river-copper-lantern-88"
- C. Your name and birth year
- D. The word "Password1"
**Answer: B** — Length and uniqueness matter most. Long passphrases are hard to crack and easy to remember.
`ISO: A.5.17 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q2.1.2** Why should each account have a different password?
- A. It looks more professional
- B. Because IT requires exactly ten characters
- C. So one breached site cannot unlock your other accounts
- D. It makes logging in faster
**Answer: C** — Reused passwords let attackers use one leak to break into many accounts (credential stuffing).
`ISO: A.8.5 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q2.1.3** What does multi-factor authentication (MFA) add?
- A. A longer password
- B. A backup of your files
- C. Faster internet
- D. A second, separate proof of identity beyond your password
**Answer: D** — MFA requires something you have or are in addition to your password, blocking most account takeovers.
`ISO: A.5.17 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q2.1.4** Your phone buzzes with an MFA approval prompt you did NOT request. What should you do?
- A. Deny it and report it — someone may have your password
- B. Approve it to stop the buzzing
- C. Ignore it and approve later
- D. Approve it and change nothing
**Answer: A** — An unexpected prompt means someone is trying to log in as you. Deny and report so the password can be changed.
`ISO: A.8.5 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

**Q2.1.5** What is a safe way to store many unique passwords?
- A. A sticky note on your monitor
- B. A reputable password manager
- C. A shared spreadsheet
- D. The same password everywhere so you remember it
**Answer: B** — A password manager generates and stores unique credentials securely so you do not have to memorise them.
`ISO: A.5.17 · Tier 1 · Foundation · Difficulty 1/3`

### Topic 2 — Passwords & multi-factor authentication: Tier 2 — Practitioner

**Q2.2.1** You are working the IT service desk. A caller says their account is locked, gives their name, staff number, and department, and asks you to reset the password and read it out over the phone. They also ask you to switch off MFA (multi-factor authentication — a second proof of identity such as an app code or a fingerprint) for an hour so they can get back in quickly. What should you do?
- A. Reset the password and read it out, since the caller knew the staff number and department and the account is clearly theirs
- B. Follow the identity-verification procedure — such as calling back on the number recorded in the staff directory or obtaining manager confirmation — before any reset, and never switch off MFA or read a password aloud
- C. Reset the password but keep MFA switched on, then text the new password to the mobile number the caller gives you
- D. Ask the caller to try a few common passwords first so you can confirm the account before logging a ticket
**Answer: B** — Resetting a password is the same as granting access to the account, so it must be treated as a high-risk action that follows the published verification steps every time. Option A is the tempting one because the caller sounds informed and friendly, but name, staff number, and department are widely visible in email signatures and on company websites, so they are not proof of identity.
`ISO: A.5.17 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q2.2.2** A finance manager travelling in Ghana loses their phone, which held the authenticator app used to approve supplier payments. They need to approve an urgent payment this afternoon and ask you what to do. Which approach is correct?
- A. Ask a colleague in finance to share their own approval code for the afternoon so the payment can go through
- B. Switch off MFA for the travelling manager's account until they are back in the office next week
- C. Create a shared "finance-backup" account with no MFA so urgent payments can always be approved
- D. Use the documented account-recovery process: verify the manager's identity strongly, issue a temporary alternative second factor, register their new device, and log and review the change afterwards
**Answer: D** — Recovery is a defined process with strong identity checks, a temporary method, and a record, which restores access without weakening everyone else's protection. Option B is the tempting quick fix, but a standing exception on a payment-approval account is exactly the gap an attacker needs, and it usually stays switched off long after the trip ends.
`ISO: A.8.5 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q2.2.3** A new HR officer needs to use the recruitment portal, which currently has one login that a colleague has been using. The colleague offers to share the username and password "just for this week". What is the best action?
- A. Request a separate named account for the new officer with only the access the role needs; if the portal genuinely allows only one login, keep the credential in the approved password vault with MFA, and record who takes it out and when
- B. Accept the shared login for the week, provided the password is changed at the end of it
- C. Use the shared login and switch on MFA so that only the two of them can sign in
- D. Use the shared login but ask the colleague to delete the password message once the new officer has saved it
**Answer: A** — Named accounts keep every action attributable to one person, and where a single login truly cannot be split, a vault with individual check-out gives the same accountability without secrets floating in chat messages. Option C is the tempting technical answer, but MFA on a shared account still cannot tell you which of the two people approved a candidate or exported a shortlist.
`ISO: A.5.17 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

**Q2.2.4** The Managing Director asks for your advice. Staff complain that the current rule — twelve characters with symbols, changed every 30 days — is impossible to remember, so people are writing passwords down. The MD suggests cutting it to eight characters with a symbol and a number, changed every 90 days. What do you recommend?
- A. Keep the twelve-character rule and the 30-day change, because complexity and frequent rotation remain the strongest password controls
- B. Remove the password requirement altogether and rely on MFA alone, since MFA stops almost every account takeover
- C. Move to longer passphrases of a few unrelated words, screen new passwords against known-breached password lists, stop routine forced changes unless there is suspicion of compromise, and keep MFA on every account
- D. Allow eight characters with a symbol and a number, changed every 90 days, as a reasonable balance between security and convenience
**Answer: C** — Length beats complexity for resisting guessing, breached-password screening blocks the passwords attackers try first, and frequent forced rotation mainly produces predictable variations and written notes. Option B is the tempting modern-sounding answer, but MFA can be bypassed by convincing sign-in pages, so the password still matters as the first layer.

**Q2.2.5** The finance manager proposes two changes to the company banking portal: adding their personal email address as the account recovery address "in case the office network is down", and keeping the one-time codes (OTPs — short codes generated for a single sign-in) in a shared WhatsApp group so any team member can approve payments when someone is on leave. What is your response?
- A. Allow the personal recovery address but refuse the shared OTP group, since recovery details are less sensitive than codes
- B. Refuse both: recovery details must stay on company-controlled, monitored channels, and each approver must hold their own second factor so every payment approval is attributable to one person
- C. Allow both, provided the WhatsApp group is deleted at the end of each month
- D. Allow the shared group because it speeds up payments, and keep a log recording which member used each code
**Answer: B** — A single shared second factor turns MFA into one secret that many people hold, and a personal mailbox for recovery puts control of a company banking account outside company systems. Option D is the tempting "we will log it" compromise, but a log written after the fact cannot prove who actually pressed approve, and the codes remain visible to anyone who ever joins the group.
`ISO: A.8.5 · Tier 2 · Applied · Difficulty 2/3`

### Topic 2 — Passwords & multi-factor authentication: Tier 3 — Advanced

**Q2.3.1** It is 7:40 am on payroll day. Nothing visible happened, and they mention it casually when they arrive. What is the correct course of action?
- A. Change the account password and continue with payroll, because one approved prompt causes no harm once the password has been changed
- B. Wait and watch the account for unusual activity, and report it only if something suspicious actually appears
- C. Report it at once and treat the account as compromised: reset the password, revoke active sessions and tokens, remove any newly added MFA methods or mail-forwarding rules, and check payroll and bank records for tampering before the run proceeds
- D. Delete the authenticator app, reinstall and re-enrol it, then carry on with the payroll run as planned
**Answer: C** — A single approval can be enough to let the attacker in and register their own devices and rules, so the account must be treated as breached and cleaned before it is used for anything as sensitive as payroll. Option A is the tempting minimal response, but changing a password alone leaves the attacker's fresh MFA registration and hidden forwarding rules active, and the change is easily worked around.

- A. Immediately reset the password to a new unique one, revoke active sessions, review the account's sign-in history and registered MFA methods, and start the incident and data-protection assessment so any 72-hour notification duty can be judged
- B. Wait until Monday and ask the administrator to change the password then, since no unauthorised activity has been seen yet
- C. Ask the administrator to change the password to a variation of the old one so that it is easier for them to remember
- D. Block internet access for the whole payroll team until the investigation has finished
**Answer: A** — Exposed credentials on an account with access to salary data are a live risk, and the clock on any notification duty starts when the company becomes aware, so containment and assessment begin immediately. **Q2.3.3** A staff member insists they signed in only through the normal Microsoft 365 page, typed their password, and approved the MFA prompt — yet the attacker still read their mailbox. What is the key lesson and correct fix?
- A. The user must have shared their MFA code, so the answer is to remind everyone never to read out codes
- B. Code- and app-based MFA can be bypassed when an attacker relays the sign-in and steals the session token, so the fix is phishing-resistant authentication such as passkeys or FIDO2 security keys (hardware keys that prove the real website is being used), plus shorter session lifetimes and monitoring for unusual sign-ins
- C. MFA is not worth the effort if it can be bypassed, so the company should rely on longer passwords instead
- D. Change the password to one twenty characters long and keep every other setting unchanged
**Answer: B** — The attacker never needed the code: they let the real service check it and then took the logged-in session, so only authentication that is bound to the genuine website closes this route. - A. Accept the risk quietly and continue as normal until the vendor upgrade arrives
- B. Set a ten-character password and rotate it every 30 days, since that is the strongest control the application allows
- C. Shut the application down until it supports MFA, regardless of the operational impact
- D. Put compensating controls around it: restrict access to a trusted network or VPN that itself requires MFA, give each user a unique long passphrase kept in the password vault, apply least privilege and logging, and record a time-limited risk exception with the upgrade date
**Answer: D** — When a system cannot meet the standard, the answer is layered controls, restricted reach, and a documented, dated decision that senior management owns. `ISO: A.8.5 · Tier 3 · Advanced · Difficulty 3/3`

What is the correct action?

**Answer: C** — Emergency access can be granted safely, but only through a defined route that keeps MFA in place and leaves a record, so genuine urgency is met without opening a permanent door. `ISO: A.5.17 · Tier 3 · Advanced · Difficulty 3/3`

- D. Forward the email to your personal account so you can examine it at home.
**Answer: C** — A failed fraud attempt is still valuable information: recording it lets security assess whether other staff received the same message and warns the wider finance team. ### Topic 7 — Security incident reporting: Tier 3 — Advanced

**Q7.3.1** It is Saturday morning. You discover that a staff member's email account sent roughly 400 messages overnight to clients, each asking them to confirm bank details on a link. The account is still signed in on an unknown device abroad. What is the correct order of action?

- D. Isolate the account first (revoke active sign-ins and reset the credentials), report through the incident process immediately, then preserve the evidence and investigate.
**Answer: D** — Containment comes before everything else: while the account is still active, every minute adds more sent mail and more exposed client data, so revoke access, report, and only then dig into causes with the evidence intact. ### Topic 8 — Malware, ransomware & safe downloads: Tier 3 — Advanced

**Q8.3.1** At 1 a.m., a staff member's phone shows seven sign-in approval prompts in a row for their work email. Earlier that evening they opened an email attachment and enabled its content so the document would display. What is the correct first action?
- A. Report immediately and, in parallel, deny the prompts and change the password from a different, trusted device — repeated unexpected approval prompts mean someone already has the password and is trying to finish the sign-in.
- B. Approve one prompt to stop the noise, then change the password in the morning.
- C. Wait until morning to see whether the prompts stop on their own.
- D. Change the password at the next convenient moment; the second check is already blocking the sign-in, so there is no urgency.
**Answer: A** — This is a multi-stage chain: the attachment delivered the malicious code, the password was captured, and the prompts are the attacker trying to complete the login, so the account must be locked down and reported at once. - A. Whether the ransom amount is small enough to pay quickly.
- B. Whether an antivirus update can decrypt the affected files.
- C. Whether staff can keep working from printed copies until Monday.

- B. Report it and leave the block in place; the security owner should verify where the file actually came from and check its digital fingerprint (a hash — a short code that proves a file is exactly the one the publisher released) before any exception is approved.
- C. Delete the utility and say nothing, since nothing bad appears to have happened.

**Answer: B** — A stolen device holding personal data is a suspected breach: it must be documented and assessed promptly, because encryption reduces risk but the organisation still has to show a considered decision, and notification deadlines do not wait for certainty. Option C is tempting because it looks customer-focused, but notifying customers without assessment risks avoidable alarm and may miss the regulator's own deadline and the technical steps such as password resets.
`ISO: A.5.12 · Tier 3 · Escalation · Difficulty 3/3`

**Channel** Email · runnable — **Topic** 10 Personal data & privacy — **ISO** A.5.34, A.5.12 — **Difficulty** High — **Roles** All
**Lure category** Payroll & HR notices

**Subject:** Annual benefits enrolment closes Friday — update your nominations
**From:** `Benefits Administration <benefits@yourco-employeeportal.com>`

> Dear {{EMPLOYEE_NAME}},
>
> The annual benefits enrolment window closes on Friday. Staff who do not update their pension and next-of-kin nominations will retain last year's elections.
>
> [Review my benefits and nominations]({{TRACKING_URL}})
>
> Benefits Administration

**Red flags** · a genuine annual process used as cover, so timing feels right · "you will retain last year's elections" sounds neutral, not threatening — which lowers suspicion · asks for next-of-kin and pension data, which is exactly the personal information attackers want · the portal is not one you have used before · the deadline is real in most organisations
**Neutral variant** · swap in the local pension regime and the local enrolment cycle.
**Teachable moment** · Attackers borrow real processes. The test is not whether the process exists, but whether the channel is the one your employer actually uses.
**Mapped training** · Topic 10, Tier 3 — Advanced

## D-24 · Customs clearance fee for an inbound shipment

**Channel** Email · runnable — **Topic** 1 Phishing & social engineering — **ISO** A.6.3, A.5.14 — **Difficulty** Medium — **Roles** Operations, logistics, procurement, finance
**Lure category** Invoice & mandate fraud

**Subject:** Shipment held at port — clearance fee outstanding
**From:** `Clearance Agent <clearance@yourco-freight-ng.com>`

> Dear {{EMPLOYEE_NAME}},
>
> Your consignment is currently held pending payment of an outstanding clearance fee. Storage charges accrue daily from tomorrow.
>
> [View charges and make payment]({{TRACKING_URL}})
>
> Reference: CN-2026-8841
> Clearing Department

**Red flags** · accruing daily charges create escalating financial pressure · a real operational headache (goods held at port) that staff want to resolve immediately · you are asked to pay through a link rather than through your clearing agent of record · a reference number that cannot be checked against any system you hold · the amount is not stated, so clicking feels like the only way to learn it
**Neutral variant** · localise the port, the currency and the reference format; the daily-accrual pressure is universal.
**Teachable moment** · Money plus a ticking clock is the strongest combination attackers have. Payments go through the process that owns them, never through a link.
**Mapped training** · Topic 1, Tier 2 — Practitioner

# Teaching-only scenario cards

These six cannot be delivered by the platform's email pipeline. They exist as teaching content: use them in briefings, in the quiz bank, and on the teachable-moment page for the relevant topic. Each is labelled clearly so nobody schedules it as a campaign.

---

## T-01 · SMS phishing (smishing): blocked card

**Channel** SMS — teaching-only — **Topic** 2 Passwords & MFA — **ISO** A.5.17, A.5.34 — **Difficulty** Medium — **Roles** All

**Message text:**

> `YOURBANK: Your debit card has been blocked due to unusual activity. Verify now to reactivate: yourbank-verify.ng/secure`

**Red flags** · your bank already knows your number, so an unannounced SMS is a weak channel · a shortened or lookalike domain · a threat to your ability to spend money · "verify now" with no reference number · text-message links cannot be inspected by hovering the way an email link can
**What to teach** · Never act on a link in an unsolicited SMS. Open your banking app directly, or call the number on your card. If the message pressures you, that is the signal.
**Mapped training** · Topic 2, Tier 2 — Practitioner

---

## T-02 · Voice phishing (vishing): the helpful service desk

**Channel** Phone call — teaching-only — **Topic** 2 Passwords & MFA — **ISO** A.5.17, A.6.3 — **Difficulty** High — **Roles** All

**Call outline:**

> "Hello, this is Daniel from the IT service desk. We're seeing failed sign-in attempts on your account. I can fix this in two minutes. I'll send you a code now — just read it back to me and I'll reset the session. Also, can you confirm your username?"

**Red flags** · an inbound call you did not request · a technical problem you cannot independently see · the caller is friendly, competent and in a hurry to help · asks for a one-time code, which no genuine support agent ever needs · asks you to confirm identity by *giving* information rather than by being verified himself
**What to teach** · Hang up and call the service desk on the number published internally. A real service desk will never object to being verified. Treat any request for a one-time code as an attack, without exception.
**Mapped training** · Topic 2, Tier 3 — Advanced

---

## T-03 · QR code phishing (quishing)

**Channel** Printed poster, sticker or email image — teaching-only — **Topic** 1 Phishing & social engineering — **ISO** A.6.3, A.8.7 — **Difficulty** Medium — **Roles** All

**Scenario:**

> A laminated notice appears in the car park and in the lift: *"New staff parking payment system. Scan to register your vehicle before Friday."* A QR code fills the lower half of the poster. Scanning it opens a mobile page branded with the company logo, asking for employee number, email and password.

**Red flags** · a QR code in a physical place cannot be inspected before you scan it · the poster pressures with a familiar deadline · the page mimics internal branding, which is easy to copy · the request for an employee number and password has no legitimate reason · stickers placed over existing codes are a known variant
**What to teach** · A QR code is just a link you cannot read. Check the web address before entering anything, and prefer typing the address yourself. Physical notices that ask for credentials are a security report, not a task.
**Mapped training** · Topic 1, Tier 2 — Practitioner

---

## T-04 · Chat-app impersonation of an executive

**Channel** WhatsApp, Telegram or similar — teaching-only — **Topic** 10 Personal data & privacy — **ISO** A.5.34, A.6.3 — **Difficulty** High — **Roles** All, priority staff with payment authority and executive assistants

**Message outline:**

> "Hi {{EMPLOYEE_NAME}}, this is the MD. I'm in a board meeting and can't take calls. I need a discreet favour — I need you to process an urgent payment today to a new supplier. Keep this between us for now, it's commercially sensitive. I'll send the account details."

**Red flags** · contact from a senior person through an unexpected, informal channel · an explicit request for secrecy, which removes your ability to check with anyone · unavailability for a call is explained pre-emptively · urgency plus a new payee · the display name says "MD" but the number is not one you have · a real executive would use the finance process, which exists precisely for this
**What to teach** · Authority plus secrecy is the signature of executive impersonation. Verify by calling the person on a number you already hold. "Keep this between us" should always increase your suspicion, never lower it.
**Mapped training** · Topic 10, Tier 3 — Advanced

---

## T-05 · USB baiting

**Channel** Physical device — teaching-only — **Topic** 9 Removable media & safe data transfer — **ISO** A.7.10, A.8.7 — **Difficulty** Low — **Roles** All

**Scenario:**

> A USB stick is found in the car park and again in the reception waiting area. It is labelled **"Payroll 2026 — CONFIDENTIAL"** with a company logo sticker. A colleague suggests plugging it in to find out who it belongs to.

**Red flags** · the label is designed to trigger both curiosity and a sense of duty · it appeared in two places within the same week, which is a deliberate placement pattern, not carelessness · company branding on removable media proves nothing · the helpful impulse ("let's find the owner") is the mechanism
**What to teach** · Never plug in unknown removable media, on any device, for any reason. Hand it to security in a sealed bag. This attack works because it recruits your helpfulness.
**Mapped training** · Topic 9, Tier 2 — Practitioner

---

## T-06 · Voice-clone authority call

**Channel** Phone or voice note — teaching-only — **Topic** 7 Security incident reporting — **ISO** A.5.24, A.6.3 — **Difficulty** High — **Roles** Finance, management, executive assistants

**Call outline:**

> The voice on the line is recognisably the Chief Financial Officer, with the right accent and cadence. "I'm travelling and my line is bad. We've had a demand from a regulator and I've authorised payment. I need you to approve the transfer now. My email is down — just use these details. Don't escalate this yet, I'll explain on Monday."

**Red flags** · a familiar voice is no longer proof of identity, because short samples of speech can now be cloned · a plausible scenario that explains every anomaly at once (bad line, travelling, email down) · a demand for immediate payment · an instruction not to escalate, which disables the control designed for exactly this moment · pre-emptive explanation of why normal verification will not work
**What to teach** · Voice is not authentication. Any payment instruction that arrives by voice alone follows the normal dual-approval process. An instruction not to escalate is a red flag in itself and should be escalated.
**Mapped training** · Topic 7, Tier 3 — Advanced

# Part F — Campaign runbook

This is the operating procedure. It assumes the platform's structural safeguards are in place and describes how the programme is run within them.

## F1 · Purpose and scope

Covers every simulated phishing campaign run under this pack: authorisation, targeting, scheduling, safety controls, escalation, data handling and reporting. It does **not** cover real incident response — but see F9, which is the interaction between the two.

## F2 · Governance and authorisation

Two structural gates exist and must both be satisfied before any email is sent. Neither can be waived by the programme lead.

| Gate | What it proves | Failure behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Consent gate** | A signed authorisation agreement exists for the tenant | Campaign creation is blocked |
| **Domain-ownership gate** | The sending domain resolves and is proven to belong to the client | Send to any address off a verified domain is refused, at add and at launch |

**Roles**

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Programme lead | Runs campaigns, owns this runbook, holds the kill switch |
| Client sponsor | Approves cadence, difficulty, notify/silent policy, exclusions |
| Client security contact | Receives reports, investigates real threats, joins escalation |
| Compliance officer | Confirms ISO evidenced controls are current, reviews records |
| Platform superadmin | Owns the shared catalogue and final approval of scenario content |

**Minimum authorisation checklist before campaign 1**
1. Signed agreement on file.
2. Employee list confirmed as current staff on client-owned work domains only.
3. Sending domain verified; SPF, DKIM and DMARC published and passing.
4. Notify or silent policy chosen and recorded (see E-04A/E-04B).
5. Exclusion list agreed and loaded.
6. Escalation contacts named with out-of-hours numbers.
7. Kill-switch operator named, with a named deputy.

## F3 · Legal and ethical framework

**Consent and notice.** Staff must have been told, in advance and in writing, that simulated phishing forms part of the security programme (E-01). A silent campaign without that standing notice is not defensible and should not be run.

**No credential capture.** The platform's landing page records only that a submission occurred and the length of the input; typed usernames and passwords are never transmitted to or stored by the server. Do not deploy any custom landing page that captures real credentials — that converts an awareness exercise into an actual credential-harvesting system and creates a genuine breach risk.

**Data protection.** Employee names, work email addresses, click/open/report outcomes and training records are personal data. Process them under the client's lawful basis for security monitoring and training, minimise what is collected, restrict access to named roles, and apply the client's retention schedule. Where the client is subject to the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) and NDPR, record the lawful basis and retain a copy of the notice given to staff.

**Labour relations.** Simulations must never be used as a performance-management instrument. No named list of clickers is published, and no click outcome is recorded against an individual's appraisal. If a client asks for one, decline — it will reduce reporting rates and increase real incident risk.

**Non-email scenarios.** The six teaching-only cards in Part D (SMS, voice, QR, chat apps, physical media) must not be represented to staff as live campaigns. They are teaching content.

## F4 · Participant rules and exclusions

**Always exclude:**
- Staff on parental, sick or other long-term leave
- Anyone who has joined within the last 30 days
- Anyone with an open HR grievance or disciplinary process
- Shared, role-based or functional mailboxes (`info@`, `accounts@`, `hr@`) — a click from one of these tells you nothing about any individual
- External parties, contractors without client-issued addresses, and former staff

**Handle with care:**
- Executive leadership. Include the CEO only with explicit sponsor approval and never in campaign 1; executives who fail publicly stop supporting the programme.
- Staff who have previously reported distress. Offer an opt-out route and honour it without requiring a reason.
- IT and security staff. They will spot the simulation; treat their reports as a positive signal, not a failed test.

## F5 · Targeting and segmentation

Match the lure to the role. A payroll confirmation sent to a software engineer measures nothing useful; the same email sent to payroll measures exactly the right thing.

| Segment | Preferred lures | Scenarios |
|---|---|---|
| Finance, accounts payable | Mandate change, invoice overdue, tax notice, clearance fee | D-01, D-11, D-13, D-20, D-24 |
| HR and payroll | CV attachment, payroll confirmation, benefits enrolment | D-12, D-19, D-23, D-03 |
| Operations and admin | Delivery, courier, customer complaint, voicemail | D-18, D-21, D-08 |
| IT | VPN, licence renewal, mailbox quota, MFA re-enrolment | D-05, D-15, D-16, D-20 |
| Compliance and legal | Data subject request, regulator notice, classification | D-10, D-13, D-04 |
| All staff | Phishing fundamentals, reporting, physical security | D-02, D-07, D-09, D-14, D-17, D-22 |

## F6 · Cadence and scheduling

**Baseline cadence:** one campaign per quarter per population, with no more than one email per person per campaign. Higher frequency breeds habituation and resentment, both of which depress reporting.

**Avoid these windows:**
- The first working day after a public holiday
- Month-end and payroll week **for non-finance lures** (you will conflate your simulation with genuine month-end stress)
- Financial year-end close and audit periods for finance staff
- The week of a redundancy announcement, restructuring or a serious business incident
- Ramadan, Easter and major public holidays — measure around cultural calendars, not through them

**Do** align a lure to the period it exploits — an invoice-run mandate change sent the day before the run is the most instructive simulation you can send to a finance team. That is deliberate realism, not opportunism, and it should be noted in the campaign record.

## F7 · Send pacing and deliverability

- Send in business hours in the recipients' own timezone, spread across the window rather than in a single burst.
- Pace delivery through the queue rather than firing simultaneously — a sudden burst from a new domain is itself a spam signal.
- Confirm the sending domain's SPF, DKIM and DMARC are all passing **before** the first send, and re-check quarterly.
- Have the client allow-list the sending domain and the tracking domain at their mail gateway, and record that they have.
- Run the delivery-test probe to a controlled mailbox before each campaign.
- Keep the log mailer available for dry runs of the full click → teachable moment → course → quiz path without sending real email.

## F8 · Difficulty ladder

| Programme stage | Lures | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| Quarter 1 | Fundamental credential harvesting and reporting | Low |
| Quarter 2 | Two or three red flags, role-relevant | Medium |
| Quarter 3 onward | Multi-stage lures, localised context, authority impersonation | High |
| Any repeat clicker | One tier above their last campaign | Escalated |

Never repeat the same lure against the same person. Recognition of a *specific* email is not the skill being trained.

## F9 · Stop criteria and kill switch

The kill switch halts queued and in-flight sends in a single action. Anyone named in F2 may call a stop; **nobody needs permission to stop a campaign.** Restarting requires sponsor sign-off.

**Stop immediately if:**
1. A real security incident is detected affecting the same population — a live campaign confuses the response and can be mistaken for the real thing.
2. Credible threat intelligence indicates the organisation is being actively targeted. Attackers have previously borrowed simulation themes to add plausibility.
3. A real phishing email is reported that closely mimics the current simulation, creating ambiguity about what is real.
4. Any participant reports serious distress, or an HR grievance is raised in connection with the campaign.
5. A client executive, client communications team or external party raises a reputational concern.
6. The sending domain's authentication begins failing, or delivery volumes trigger gateway throttling.
7. Volume of reports exceeds the security team's capacity to triage, so that a genuine report could be lost in the queue.

**Distinguishing a simulation from a real attack — the critical rule.** During a campaign window, assume any reported message **could be real**. Triage every report as though it were genuine. A simulation that causes a real attack to be dismissed as "just the test" is the single worst outcome this programme can produce. Simulation emails should be identifiable to the security team from the tracking token even when they are not identifiable to staff.

## F10 · Data handling and retention

| Data | Handling |
|---|---|
| Click, open and submit events | Access limited to programme lead and security contact |
| Submitted content | Not collected — a length value only. No password is ever transmitted |
| Training and quiz records | Retained as evidence of competence; may be shared with the individual |
| Named clicker lists | **Not produced.** Aggregate by department and role only |
| Campaign records | Retained per client retention schedule, with the notify/silent decision recorded |

## F11 · Metrics and thresholds

Track four numbers. Report them as trends against your own previous campaigns, never against an unattributed industry benchmark.

| Metric | What it means | Healthy direction |
|---|---|---|
| Click rate | How many acted on the lure | Down over time |
| Submission rate | How many entered credentials — the serious outcome | Down, and should approach zero |
| Report rate | How many flagged it — **the metric that matters most** | Up, every quarter |
| Training completion | Whether the loop actually closed after a click | Up, toward 100% |

Interpret with care. A very low click rate sometimes reflects manager pre-warning, not awareness. A falling report rate is a warning sign even if the click rate also falls. Read the two together.

## F12 · Post-campaign process

1. Stop and close the campaign; confirm no sends remain queued.
2. Confirm every clicker was routed to the correct training module and has a completion path.
3. Send the management report (E-14) within five working days.
4. Send reporting praise (E-12) to everyone who reported.
5. Update the ISO evidence pack with the campaign record and completion figures.
6. Review what to change — including whether the programme itself caused friction — at the quarterly review (E-15).
7. Record the campaign in the log: date, lures, segments, notify policy, results, any stops called and why.

## F13 · Pre-flight checklist

- [ ] Signed authorisation on file
- [ ] Domain ownership verified
- [ ] SPF, DKIM and DMARC passing
- [ ] Sending and tracking domains allow-listed by the client, confirmed in writing
- [ ] Employee list current; exclusions applied and spot-checked
- [ ] Notify or silent policy chosen and recorded
- [ ] Lures matched to role segments
- [ ] Sending window checked against business and cultural calendars
- [ ] Routing rules confirmed — every lure maps to a training module
- [ ] Escalation contacts and out-of-hours numbers confirmed
- [ ] Kill-switch operator and deputy named
- [ ] Delivery test completed
- [ ] No credential capture on any landing page
- [ ] Sponsor sign-off obtained

# Part G — ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A crosswalk

## An important distinction, stated plainly

This pack is **evidence of awareness and training**. It supports awareness obligations; it does not implement the operational controls it teaches people about.

- For **A.6.3 (Information security awareness, education and training)**, this pack is direct evidence: it shows a defined curriculum, delivery, assessment and completion records.
- For every other control listed below, the pack **educates staff about** the control. The control itself must still be implemented, operated and evidenced by the organisation — a firewall is not built by a quiz, and a classification scheme is not created by a training video.

Stating this accurately matters. An auditor who finds a training folder presented as evidence that access control is implemented will treat the whole statement of applicability with more suspicion, not less.

## How to present this to an auditor

1. **Curriculum** — the ten-topic spine, with the control each topic supports (above).
2. **Delivery** — completion records per person, per module, with dates.
3. **Assessment** — quiz scores and pass marks, scored server-side, with retakes permitted.
4. **Simulation evidence** — campaign records showing lure, segment, outcome rates and the corrective training automatically assigned.
5. **Improvement** — campaign-over-campaign trend in click and report rates, plus the quarterly review minutes.

Item 5 is the one most organisations are missing. ISO 27001 asks for continual improvement; a single campaign shows activity, a trend shows improvement.

## H3 · Assumptions

1. **Question count and provenance.** Of the 150 questions, the 50 in Tier 1 across all ten topics are the questions that already existed in the pack file; 100 are newly authored for Tiers 2 and 3. Tier 1 wording and answers are unchanged. **One defect in the source was corrected:** the legacy Tier 1 bank keyed 38 of its 50 answers to option B, so a learner who always chose B would have scored 80% — a pass — on several quizzes. Option order has been rebalanced across all 50 items so that guessing now scores at chance (the key is now A 13 / B 13 / C 12 / D 12). No prompt, option text, answer or explanation was altered to achieve this. If you regenerate Tier 1 from `iso27001-2022.ts`, the skew returns.
2. **Tier naming.** Tier 1 Foundation, Tier 2 Practitioner, Tier 3 Advanced. Tier 1 is the version publicly marketed.
3. **Classification labels.** Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted — taken from the existing pack's video script. **Confirm these match the client's actual policy**, since the quiz content depends on them.
4. **Merge fields.** Only `{{EMPLOYEE_NAME}}` and `{{TRACKING_URL}}` are known to be supported. Every other field in Part E is proposed and needs a template layer that supports it.
5. **Report triage SLA.** "Reviewed within one working day" in E-12 is a proposed service level, not an existing commitment.

## H4 · Uncertainties requiring your decision or verification

1. **Notify or silent campaign policy** (E-04A / E-04B). Not a content question — a governance one. Must be recorded before campaign 1.
2. **Labour law.** Whether unannounced simulated phishing against staff requires prior notice or works-council consultation in your jurisdiction was **not verified**. Take local advice before running a silent policy.
3. **Privacy law references.** The narrative sections deliberately say "the relevant regulator" and "the legal deadline" rather than asserting statutory detail. There is one exception: quiz item **Q7.3.2** states the 72-hour notification expectation and names the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) and the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA). That claim was verified during authoring, against the sources in H5: NDPA 2023 **s.40(2)** requires a data controller to notify the Commission within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach likely to risk individuals' rights and freedoms, and **s.40(1)** requires a processor to notify the controller — which is why the correct answer is to report internally at once rather than wait for proof of harm. The NDPR 2019 was superseded by the NDPA 2023. **Have counsel confirm the wording and the current NDPC filing channel** before this item is used in a record relied on for compliance.
4. **Physical scenarios in tenant-controlled premises.** The tailgating and visitor scenarios assume the client controls the building and reception. If premises are shared, landlord rules apply and the scenarios need adapting.
5. **Gateway allow-listing.** Delivery assumes the client's mail gateway allow-lists the sending and tracking domains. Not assumed to be automatic.
6. **Behavioural figures.** The "one in three people click" line in E-05 is an industry-typical figure used for reassurance. It is **not a measurement of your organisation**. Replace it with your own baseline once campaign 1 reports.

# Appendix 2 — Metrics and thresholds

## Suggested target bands

Use as starting points for a first programme. They are conventional awareness-programme ranges, **not benchmarks attributable to a specific study**, and should be replaced with your own baseline after two campaigns.

| Metric | Baseline expectation | 12-month target | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Click rate | 20–35% showing some response | Below 10% | Any rise quarter on quarter |
| Submission rate | 5–15% | Below 3% | Any submission on a finance-targeted lure |
| Report rate | 5–15% | Above 40% | **A fall in report rate**, even if clicks also fall |
| Training completion after a click | 40–70% | Above 90% | Below 50% within 30 days |
| Quiz pass rate, Tier 1 | 75–90% | Above 90% | A module failing consistently — fix the module, not the staff |
| Repeat clickers within 90 days | 10–20% of clickers | Below 10% | Repeat clicks tracking to one department |

## Reading the numbers honestly

- **A low click rate is not automatically good.** Manager pre-warning, an allow-listed domain that looks internal, or a lure that was simply too obvious all depress the figure without improving awareness.
- **Report rate is the leading indicator.** Organisations with high reporting catch real attacks early. Prioritise it over click rate in every management conversation.
- **A consistently failed quiz indicates a bad question**, not a poor cohort. Review the item before running a remediation campaign.
- **Never compare yourself to a vendor's headline statistic.** Compare this quarter to last quarter, on your own population, with your own lures.
